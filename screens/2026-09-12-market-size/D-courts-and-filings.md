# Supplier quality chargebacks: court records, bankruptcy filings, SEC filings

Scope: real dollar amounts and counts of supplier quality chargebacks in manufacturing (automotive first, then appliance and heavy equipment), from legal and financial records only. Search-engine summaries were not treated as evidence; every entry below was fetched and read as a document. First pass (quality manuals, surveys, forums, vendor blogs) already ruled out and not repeated here.

Access note: CourtListener opinion pages returned an AWS WAF challenge to direct fetch (HTTP 202, `x-amzn-waf-action: challenge`, zero-byte body) and Justia returned HTTP 403. Full opinion text below was retrieved through the `r.jina.ai` read-through proxy, which returns the same public opinion text. Kroll/Stretto-hosted bankruptcy claims portals (cases.ra.kroll.com) render their docket tables via client-side JavaScript, which is invisible to a non-executing fetch; those returned "No Record(s) Found" even though dockets exist, so I could not get past the landing page to actual first-day declarations or claims-register entries.

## What could not be found and why

1. **No single all-in industry dollar figure or count of supplier quality chargebacks exists in any legal or financial record found.** Every VERIFIED number above is a single company's, single period's, or single dispute's figure. Nothing aggregates across suppliers, OEMs, or the industry. This matches the first pass's finding from quality manuals and surveys.

2. **Court opinions almost never state a running total or a count of chargeback incidents.** Judges quote the specific debit or debit memo at issue in the count before them (ATD: $94,000 and $709,971.86; Alpine Machine: $9,767.10 and $2,785.10) or, at most, a rough order of magnitude for background color (Mando: "hundreds of thousands" then "several million"), because the chargeback total is rarely itself the disputed legal question, more often it's a fact recited on the way to a contract, conversion, retaliation, or bankruptcy-setoff ruling.

3. **Bankruptcy claims registers were the most promising source in theory and the least accessible in practice.** Kroll's and Stretto's free case-information sites (cases.ra.kroll.com, primeclerk.com, etc.) exist specifically so the public can browse first-day declarations and claims registers, but their docket tables are populated by client-side JavaScript that a text-only fetch cannot execute, so the tool returned "No Record(s) Found" even on real, populated dockets. Getting the actual first-day declaration (which is where a debtor's own counsel usually explains customer accommodation programs and any offset/chargeback exposure in narrative form) or a claims-register line item filed by GM, Ford, or Stellantis against Shiloh, Unique Fabricating, or Dura would require PACER access or a JavaScript-rendering browser tool, neither of which was available in this pass.

4. **CourtListener's opinion pages are behind an AWS WAF bot challenge**, and Justia returned HTTP 403 to a direct fetch. This was worked around using a public read-through proxy (r.jina.ai) for the specific opinions above, but it means any case not independently mirrored on Justia/Leagle/CourtListener could have been missed if the proxy path failed silently.

5. **SEC full-text search only reliably surfaced one usable line item across nine target companies (Core Molding Technologies).** Sypris Solutions, Stoneridge, and Commercial Vehicle Group all use chargeback/setoff/recoupment vocabulary somewhere in their filings, but only as boilerplate accounting-policy or lending-covenant language with no dollar amount attached, or as a description of a favorable internal cost trend rather than a customer-imposed charge. Strattec Security, Superior Industries, Motorcar Parts of America, and Shiloh Industries produced no relevant hits at all on any of the practitioner terms searched. This is consistent with quality chargebacks typically being immaterial enough, relative to these companies' total revenue, that they do not require separate quantified disclosure under materiality thresholds, Core Molding is the exception because its accounting policy note happens to itemize the chargeback allowance specifically rather than folding it into a general bad-debt reserve.
