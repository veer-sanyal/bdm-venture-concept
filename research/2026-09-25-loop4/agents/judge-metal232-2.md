**VERDICT: PASS** on the concept as pitched. The rule is real and the proof burden is real. But the money sits in a small, unmeasured group of products near the 15% line, the chosen customer can already work out metal weight from its own CAD files, and the rule has been rewritten twice in three months.

## Claim check against primary sources

- **Full-value tariffs from April 6, 2026: confirmed.** The April 2 proclamation charges duty on the whole customs value. Annex I-A (basic metal goods) pays 50%. Annex I-B (manufactured goods) pays 25%. Annex III (industrial and electrical equipment) pays at least 15% until the end of 2027. ([Thompson Coburn](https://www.thompsoncoburn.com/insights/74-april-2-2026-new-proclamation-makes-major-revisions-to-section-232-tariffs-on-steel-aluminum-copper/), [Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/04/09/2026-06960/strengthening-actions-taken-to-adjust-imports-of-aluminum-steel-and-copper-into-the-united-states))
- **Under 15% metal by weight pays 0%, and the metal weight in kg goes on every line: confirmed.** CBP's guidance says importers claiming code 9903.82.03 must "report the aggregate weight of the applicable metal(s) in kg as a second quantity on the entry summary line" ([CSMS 68253075](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USDHSCBP/bulletins/4117593)). The pitch leaves out one thing: goods classified in HTS chapters 72, 73, 74 and 76 (steel, iron or steel articles, copper, aluminum) cannot use the exemption.
- **The rule moves a lot.** A June 8 proclamation cut rates on about 60 codes, added four, and created temporary categories that end in 2028 ([CSMS 68855869](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USDHSCBP/bulletins/41aa83d)).
- **$227B: confirmed, but it is not the market.** Global Trade Alert counts $227B of 2024 imports across 326 Annex I-B codes ([GTA](https://globaltradealert.org/blog/s232-metals-restructuring-april-2026)). That is the import value of every product in the group, and most were listed because they are mostly metal. Nobody has published how much sits near the 15% line.
- **No official method for weighing.** CBP's Base Metals Center has said nothing on how to measure the weight, whether packaging counts, or whether drawings and bills of materials are acceptable proof ([GHY summary](https://www.ghy.com/trade-compliance/cbp-base-metals-center-updated-guidance-on-section-232-content-reporting/)). Law firms expect closer CBP scrutiny of low-metal claims ([Foley](https://www.foley.com/insights/publications/2026/05/what-every-multinational-should-know-about-the-new-rules-for-section-232-tariffs-on-steel-aluminum-and-copper-derivatives/)).
- **Enforcement risk is real but not yet tested on this rule.** The DOJ Trade Fraud Task Force reports over $1B recovered in about 10 months, including a $549.5M aluminum case ([ArentFox](https://www.afslaw.com/perspectives/investigations-blog/baseline-not-finish-line-fca-customs-enforcement-mid-year-2026)). I found no enforcement case yet about a 15% weight claim.

## Who else serves this customer

- **Supplier data and where the metal was melted:** Assent already runs supplier surveys and tracing back to the smelter, and it bought IPOINT in July 2026 ([Assent](https://www.assent.com/blog/prepare-for-section-232-tariffs/)). Assent says it helps with CBP paperwork such as mill test certificates. IPOINT is a materials-compliance firm; I'm inferring, not verified, that it brings part-level material and weight data. Z2Data sells sub-tier tracing of melt, pour, smelt and cast ([Z2Data](https://www.z2data.com/insights/why-you-need-supply-chain-visibility-for-section-232/)). GoSmarter reads mill certificates with AI.
- **Customs filing and trade software:** ONESOURCE, Descartes and e2open, plus brokers such as C.H. Robinson and Expeditors.
- **Weight from CAD:** CAD programs already calculate mass by material, so this step is close to free for a company that owns its drawings.

**Does an incumbent own the data or buying channel?** Partly. The engineering data sits in the customer's own CAD and product-data systems, and no customs vendor owns it. The supplier-data channel belongs to Assent and Z2Data at mid-size and larger manufacturers. The filing channel belongs to brokers. I found no company whose pitch matches this one, so there is no "same team" case.

## 1. Strongest version

Drop the yearly fee on every product and drop Indiana. Become a service that finds and defends products near the 15% line, sold through customs brokers and trade lawyers.

- Scan an importer's products in the manufactured-goods and equipment groups (Annexes I-B and III). Find those between about 8% and 25% metal by weight, and check the figures against the supplier's parts list and a physical teardown.
- For products below the line, build the proof file.
- For products just above it, design the redesign or material change that moves them below, and charge a share of the duty saved.
- Leave melt-country tracing to Assent and Z2Data through integrations.
- Pick targets by product category, not state: small appliances, lighting, furniture, power tools, HVAC parts.

## 3. What kills it, and the fastest test

**What kills it:**
- Few products actually sit in the 8–25% band.
- Importers' own engineers can produce the numbers and CBP accepts them.
- A proclamation changes or removes the 15% rule.

**Fastest test:** Offer 2–3 customs brokers a free near-line scan for their clients. Take five importers' entry summary reports from their CBP (ACE) accounts for April to September 2026, plus weight data for each product. Add up the duty paid, or exemption claimed, on products between 8% and 25% metal.
- If the typical importer has less than about $150K a year of duty at stake in that band, kill it.
- Also kill it if fewer than 2 of the 5 will pay for redesign work or a defense file.

This takes about two weeks and needs real customer data. Desk research cannot answer it.
