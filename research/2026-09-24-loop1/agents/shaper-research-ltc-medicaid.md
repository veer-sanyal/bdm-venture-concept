I checked the idea's two headline claims. The retroactive coverage cut is real, but it is smaller and narrower than stated. The Indiana figure is real but has nothing to do with eligibility. The biggest finding is that a small funded startup, Medicaidsoft, already sells almost exactly this product to care facilities.

## 1. The retroactive coverage change

- **What the law does.** Section 71112 of H.R. 1 (P.L. 119-12) amends 42 U.S.C. §1396a(a)(34). "Retroactive coverage" means Medicaid pays bills from months before the application. The change applies to applications filed after Dec 31, 2026, so it starts Jan 1, 2027.
  - ACA expansion adults (under 65, not on Medicare, income up to 133% of the poverty line) go from 3 months to 1.
  - Everyone else, including aged, blind, disabled and nursing home applicants, goes from 3 months to 2.
  - Sources: [King & Spalding](https://www.kslaw.com/news-and-insights/the-one-big-beautiful-bill-act-explained-a-detailed-review-of-key-changes-for-the-healthcare-industry), [Justice in Aging](https://justiceinaging.org/hr1-reduces-medicaid-retroactive-eligibility-starting-in-2027/).
- **The pitch's causal story is wrong.** Coverage is counted back from the month the application is filed, not the month it is approved ([Justice in Aging](https://justiceinaging.org/hr1-reduces-medicaid-retroactive-eligibility-starting-in-2027/)). A slow caseworker costs the facility nothing under this rule. The lost month comes only when the facility files more than 2 months after the resident needed coverage. So the sales message is "file inside the window", not "get approved faster".
- **The $9,450 a month figure is the private-pay price, not the facility's loss.** The median private semi-private room costs $315/day, or $9,581/month ([CareScout 2025](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260302283881/en/CareScout-Releases-2025-Cost-of-Care-Survey-Results)). What the facility actually loses on a Medicaid-eligible resident is closer to the Medicaid rate. That averaged $198 per resident per day across 44 states, about $6,000/month ([ASPE](https://aspe.hhs.gov/reports/assessing-medicaid-payments-costs-nursing-homes)). The loss is also smaller because the resident's own income still goes to the facility.
- **Some states already cut retroactive coverage by waiver** (Florida, Iowa and others). Many of those waivers kept it for aged and disabled enrollees ([CoveredUSA](https://coveredusa.org/en/qa/medicaid-retroactive-eligibility-by-state)). I could not confirm state by state which nursing home populations were affected.
- **Other H.R. 1 provisions that add eligibility work.** Only the first one touches nursing home residents directly.
  - Home equity cap for long-term care eligibility, starting Jan 1, 2028 (§71108).
  - Address verification, starting 2027 (§71103).
  - Enrollment-streamlining rules frozen until Oct 1, 2034 (§§71101-71102), which keeps the paperwork burden where it is.
  - A new home and community care waiver option, starting July 1, 2028 (§71121).
  - The 6-month redeterminations (§71107) apply to expansion adults only. Nursing home residents stay on annual review ([King & Spalding](https://www.kslaw.com/news-and-insights/the-one-big-beautiful-bill-act-explained-a-detailed-review-of-key-changes-for-the-healthcare-industry)).

## 2. The Indiana $462M is not an eligibility backlog

The $462M owed to 496 nursing homes is two stalled quarterly facility payments, out of about $1B a year. They stalled because CMS had not approved Indiana's FY2026 payment method during the move to managed care (PathWays for Aging). The reporting never mentions pending applications ([Skilled Nursing News](https://skillednursingnews.com/2026/04/nursing-homes-in-state-owed-almost-500m-in-medicaid-back-payments-due-to-managed-care-transition-delays/), [Indiana Capital Chronicle](https://indianacapitalchronicle.com/2026/04/08/providers-wait-for-hundreds-of-millions-in-delayed-medicaid-payments/)). This number should not be used as evidence for the idea.

What processing-time data I found:

- **Federal deadline.** States must decide within 45 days, or 90 days when a disability determination is needed.
- **Illinois audit, calendar years 2021-2023** ([digest PDF](http://www.auditor.illinois.gov/Audit-Reports/Performance-Special-Multi/Performance-Audits/2025_Releases/25-Medicaid-LTC-Eligibility-Perf-Digest.pdf)):
  - The state received 44,661 long-term care applications over the 3 years, 15,645 of them in 2023.
  - In a sample of 50, 22% ran past the deadline, and the average was 41 days.
  - Cases referred for asset investigation ran 14 to 156 days overdue.
- **Earlier Illinois backlog.** About 7,000 cases were pending, some for a year. Facilities were fronting $300M of care until a court order made the state pay after 45 days ([Healthcare Finance News](https://www.healthcarefinancenews.com/news/illinois-medicaid-backlog-spawns-multiple-lawsuits-behalf-providers-beneficiaries), [Effingham Daily News](https://www.effinghamdailynews.com/news/local_news/illinois-on-hook-for-m-in-nursing-home-care/article_39be6dca-7b42-11e8-88a6-1b480097c532.html)). Illinois now grants "provisional eligibility" and pays automatically after 45 days ([IDHS](https://www.dhs.state.il.us/page.aspx?item=115419)), which blunts the pain there.
- **Industry claim, not independently checked.** A planning site says nursing home applicants are approved in 79 days on average, and home and community waiver applicants in 89 days, based on more than 500 clients ([search summary](https://www.eldercareresourceplanning.org/medicaid-faq/applying/timing/); the page returned a 403 when I tried to read it).
- **Medicaid's share.** Medicaid is the primary payer for 63% of nursing home residents as of July 2025 ([Medicare Advocacy](https://medicareadvocacy.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/Issue-Brief-Medicaid-and-Nursing-Homes.pdf)). It paid 44% of the $147B spent on institutional long-term care in 2023 ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/5-key-facts-about-nursing-facilities-and-medicaid/)).
- **Not verified.** I found no credible national number for Medicaid-pending receivables or bad-debt write-offs. The Sparkco blog cites figures such as "$20-30k per pending resident (AHCA)", but its contact number is a 555 placeholder and it gives no sources. Discard it.

## 3. Volume

- About 15,000 certified nursing facilities and 1.2M residents as of July 2024. About 1.3M people used Medicaid nursing facility care during 2021 ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/5-key-facts-about-nursing-facilities-and-medicaid/)).
- **New applications per year: my estimate, not a published figure.** I found no national count. Illinois processed 15,645 in 2023, and that count includes supportive living facilities. Illinois has about 3.8% of the US population, which scales to roughly 300k-400k long-term care applications a year nationally. That is about 20-25 per facility per year.
- About 1.8M people received home and community care through 1915(c) waivers in 2022, across 267 waivers in 2025 ([CRS R48519](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R48519)). I found no annual count of new waiver applications.

## 4. How it is done today and what it costs

- **In-house.** The facility's business office manager does the work. I did not look up salary data.
- **Outsourced firms.** None of them publish prices: Richter (90+ staff, [site](https://www.richterhc.com/medicaid-consulting)), Medicaid Done Right (Florida, founded 2012, [site](https://www.medicaiddoneright.com/Company.html)), Medicaid Plus (PA/NJ/NY/MD/DE, flat fees, [site](https://www.mymedicaidplus.com/nursing-facilities.html)), CT Medicaid Specialists ("low fixed cost", [site](https://www.ctmedicaidspecialists.com/)). I found nothing on NCS or American Medicaid Specialists.
- **Only price points found.** A New Jersey elder law attorney charges families $6,500-8,500 for planning plus the application. He says non-attorney application firms charge about $9,000 to apply only ([Callinan, 2024](https://www.eldercarelawyer.com/blog/applying-for-medicaid-cost-and-services/)). That is one self-interested source.

## 6. My read

- **Best first paying customer.** A 10-50 building nursing home chain in a state without provisional eligibility, where the regional business office director owns the pending-receivables number. The Illinois-style automatic payment removes the pain, so avoid those states.
- **What they would pay.** No public prices exist. My guess is $300-1,000 per building per month, or $300-1,500 per application. That is far below the roughly $9,000 families reportedly pay outside firms.
- **Market size (my assumptions, not sourced):**
  - Software: 300-400k applications a year at $500 each is about $150-200M.
  - Displacing outside firms at $1,500-3,000 per application would make it $0.5-1B.
  - Assisted living and home care waivers add to that but are unquantified.
- **Biggest risk: it already exists.** Medicaidsoft ships the exact feature set (bank statements, flagged transfers, state forms, checklists) with a channel to 4,000 elder law attorneys. Telos files applications in Texas, and ExaCare owns admissions with $30M.
- **Other risks:**
  - State-by-state rules and forms.
  - Liability if a missed transfer triggers a penalty period or a fraud referral.
  - The Jan 2027 cut rewards filing faster, which a checklist and a deadline alert also solve.
- **Bottom line.** Without a clear edge over Medicaidsoft, this is a crowded niche with a weaker urgency story than claimed.
