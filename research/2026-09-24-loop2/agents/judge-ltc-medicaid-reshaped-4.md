I recommend passing. The main problem is that the pitch blames the wrong delay. The retroactive cut punishes facilities that file late, not a slow state review, and a company that fixes late filing looks like a services business, not a venture-scale one.

## Checking the key claims

- **Illinois 58 vs. 22 days: the numbers are real but thin.** The July 2025 Illinois Auditor General audit sampled 50 of 15,645 long-term-care applications from 2023. The 17 cases sent for asset investigation spent an average of 58 days at the investigating office. The other 33 took 22 days from application to decision. Those measure different things, and the samples are tiny. Seven of the 17 referrals came after the state had already approved the case. The typical application took 41 days, and 22% ran past the legal deadline.
- **The January 2027 retroactive cut is correct.** Section 71112 of Public Law 119-21 limits retroactive coverage to 2 months before the application month for non-expansion enrollees, which includes most nursing home residents. It applies to applications dated January 1, 2027 or later (California's implementing letter, ACWDL 26-07).
- **"Delays now cost unpaid care outright" is wrong about which delay.** The 2-month window counts back from the filing date. A slow state review delays payment but does not shrink coverage. Facilities lose money outright only when they file late, or when a denial for missing documents forces them to start over with a new filing date. In one case study, a 25-building chain's pending write-offs came mostly from late submissions and scattered paperwork, not state review.
- **Illinois softens the pain in the pitch's own example.** Illinois automatically grants provisional eligibility, and pays the facility, once an application has been pending 45 days.
- **The state already pulls bank data.** Federal law requires every state to run an electronic asset verification system that searches banks for account balances and transfers. What slows the facility down is getting five years of statements out of families. Plaid-style connections return only about 24 months, and older paper records must be requested from the bank.

## 1. The strongest version

Narrow it to a service that guarantees on-time, complete filing for 10 to 50 building chains, run by a small team with AI doing the back-office work. Focus on states with long processing times and no provisional eligibility.

- **Triggers:** it starts a file the day a resident's private funds or Medicare days are running out, and files well inside the 2-month window.
- **First submission is complete:** the look-back is reconciled and every entry links to its source, so caseworker requests, denials and refilings go down.
- **Rescue work:** it takes over the chain's oldest pending and denied cases.
- **Price:** charge only when a case is approved, and sell it against the chain's own write-off numbers.
- **What to drop:** "flag transfers before admission." Most admissions come straight from hospitals, and five years of statements can't be collected in that time.

## 3. What would kill it, and the fastest test

**What kills it:**
- Chains' Medicaid losses turn out to come mainly from families not cooperating or residents who were never eligible, which AI reading doesn't fix. Late filing and incomplete packets would have to be the main cause for this to work.
- Chains won't pay about $1,250 per case when a family-paid service costs them nothing.

**Fastest test (about 2 weeks):** ask the revenue leads at 8 to 10 chains of this size for their last 12 months of Medicaid-pending write-offs and denials, broken down by cause, and ask each for a signed letter of intent at $1,250 per approval. Kill the idea if late filing and missing-document denials account for less than about $30,000 per building per year, or if fewer than 2 chains sign. That is roughly the per-building fee the service would charge.
