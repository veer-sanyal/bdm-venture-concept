I found no company pitching this exact business, so the verdict below judges the idea on its own. The core facts hold up. The weak points are who chooses the commissioning agent, how much time software can really save, and how big the fee pool is.

**Checking the claims**
- **$50.7B, up 27%: confirmed.** Census put April 2026 data center construction at a $50.7B annual rate, up from $39.8B a year earlier (27–28%, depending on the revision). I read this through Bloomberg and trade reports; the census.gov page I tried didn't show the series.
- **$200k per MW per month: plausible, but at the high end.** CBRE quotes $190–235 per kW per month in Northern Virginia and Chicago, but only for small leases of 250–500 kW. It gives no dollar figure for 10 MW-plus leases, only a 6.7% rise in H1. Vacancy is 1.4% and 80.4% of capacity under construction is already pre-leased, so a late handover costs real rent.
- **Scarce commissioning agents: only secondary evidence.** Staffing surveys and recruiter reports say it; I found no primary data.
- **Fee pool (my estimate).** A secondary source puts commissioning at 1–3% of construction cost. That implies roughly $0.5–1.5B a year in US commissioning fees.

**Who else serves this customer**
- **Commissioning firms, being bought by contractors.** Limbach bought CYMCOR on Aug 4, 2026 for about $30M, even though CYMCOR oversees more than $8B of projects. Salute is taking over T5's operations arm (close announced for Sept 1, 2026) and already sells liquid-cooling commissioning support to Applied Digital and Compass. That price suggests commissioning-services firms sell cheaply.
- **Workflow software.** CxAlloy (owned by Trinity), Facility Grid, BlueRithm and CxPlanner hold the checklists and issue logs. CxPlanner's "CxAI" already writes test scripts, and BlueRithm has AI checklist tools. On what I could read, none of them grades live building-management or power-monitoring data or signs off as the agent.
- **ArchiLabs** (links to Y Combinator on its site; I didn't confirm) sells design software for data centers. It writes about auto-generated test procedures and pass/fail reports from building-management data, but mostly as what its platform could enable.
- **Aggreko** rents load banks and already offers liquid-cooled units, test plans, data capture and reports.

**Does an incumbent own the data or channel?** Nobody owns the data. The building-management and power-monitoring data belongs to the owner and its controls vendors. The buying channel is held by the incumbent commissioning firms through their relationships and service agreements with owners.

**1. Strongest version**
Aim at the general contractor's side of commissioning for liquid-cooled halls built for AI cloud providers and their developers. Don't compete to be the owner's independent agent. Contractors carry penalties for late delivery and are short of commissioning managers, and they don't need an independent party. The company would:
- bring its own data-capture kit that pulls in building-management, power-monitoring, cooling-unit and load-bank data;
- write the test scripts;
- grade each test step live;
- hand over a signed test report within 48 hours;
- charge per megawatt with a turnaround guarantee.

Over time, the data from commissioning becomes the baseline for running the building afterwards.

| Risk | 2 | Owners pick commissioning agents from firms they already trust, the signature carries liability, and the business rides one capital-spending cycle. |

**3. What kills it, and the fastest test**
It dies if owners and tenants won't accept a new agent of record, or won't let one senior agent cover several halls remotely. Without that, the leverage story disappears.

The fastest test is to put a priced offer in front of 15 construction or commissioning leads at AI cloud providers, colocation developers and data center contractors, for halls breaking ground in the next six months. Ask who approves the agent and whether they'll add this company to the bid list. If fewer than 3 of 15 say yes, stop. This takes two to three weeks.
