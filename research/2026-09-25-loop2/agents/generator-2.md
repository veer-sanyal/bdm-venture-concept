I recommend **an AI agent that files and fights warranty claims for heavy-equipment dealers** (farm, construction and truck), starting with Indiana farm-equipment dealers. This is desk research only; none of it counts as customer validation. My web-search allowance ran out partway through, so the competitor check for the equipment-dealer niche is thinner than I'd like, and a few numbers below are unverified.

## Why this one

- **How it works:** a dealer does a repair the manufacturer has to pay for. A technician's rough notes, the hours on the machine, photos and the part numbers have to become a claim that meets that manufacturer's rules. Claims get rejected for paperwork gaps even when the repair was right, and the dealer absorbs the cost.
- **The product:** the agent pulls those pieces together, writes the claim to each manufacturer's rules, checks it before it goes in, resubmits rejected claims and appeals charge-backs.
- **What they charge:** a fee per claim plus a cut of money recovered. Dealers pay nothing unless they get paid, which makes the first sale easy.
- **Customer need:** the money involved is large. US-based public manufacturers paid about $30.4B in warranty claims in 2025. Dealers depend on service and warranty income more when equipment sales are weak.
- **Why now:** manufacturers are rolling out AI to judge claims faster and more strictly (Circuitry.ai, Tavant and Annata sell exactly this). Dealers have no AI of their own on their side. That makes a clean pitch line.
- **Room in the market:** the dealer-side AI tools I found are almost all for car dealers: Forge, Reflolabs and WarrantyWriter. WarrantyWriter charges $10–50 a month just to write the claim text, so that piece is cheap. The value is in doing the whole job and being paid on results. I found no funded company doing this for equipment dealers. The Summer 2026 Y Combinator batch had none either; its nearest company was Kebra, "AI agents for field service shops."
- **Start narrow, grow broad:**
  1. Farm-equipment dealers for one or two brands in Indiana.
  2. All heavy-equipment and truck dealers.
  3. RV (Elkhart, Indiana builds most US RVs), boat and powersports dealers, then HVAC and appliance repair shops.
  4. The whole back office of any repair business that gets paid by a manufacturer.
- **A second product, unverified:** many states are said to have dealer laws requiring warranty labor to be paid at the dealer's retail rate. Armatus built a large business filing these for car dealers. Whether equipment dealers have the same rights needs checking with a lawyer or the Equipment Dealers Association.
- **Fit for Veer and Cole:** there are dealers within driving distance of campus. The fastest proof is to take one dealer's last 90 days of rejected claims, resubmit them and report the dollars recovered by the December final. The judges score customer discovery (the 2025 edition had a "Best Customer Discovery" prize). Last year's winners were also AI for unglamorous industries: trade contractors, legacy factory machines and plaintiff law firms.

**What's weakest:**
- **Market size is the likely judge objection.** My guess of roughly 20,000 non-car dealer locations at about $1–3K a month (about $0.25–0.8B a year) comes from memory, not sources. The pitch has to lean on the grow-broad path.
- **Manufacturer lock-in.** Their dealer contracts may bar third parties from logging into warranty portals, and Deere is especially controlling. The workaround is to have dealer staff click submit, or connect through the dealer's own management software. They should confirm this early.
- **The problem itself is unproven.** I couldn't find reliable rejection rates for equipment dealers; vendors' claims had no sources. If rejections are low, the recovery pitch shrinks and it becomes a harder labor-savings pitch.
- **Big dealer groups have in-house warranty staff, and manufacturers could build dealer tools themselves.**

## Runners-up worth keeping

**1. An AI quality engineer for small auto and aerospace parts suppliers.** It prepares the approval paperwork big customers demand before a part can ship (PPAP and first-article inspection reports) and the problem-and-fix reports they demand after a defect.
- *Why it could work:* the market is broad (every certified manufacturer, and Indiana is very manufacturing-heavy). Quality engineers are scarce. Purdue's Manufacturing Extension Partnership could be a route to customers.
- *Weakest:* it's already contested. QualityEngineer.ai checks PPAP packages, and incumbents 1factory and Omnex are adding AI. The pitch saves labor rather than recovering cash, so it's a harder sale. Manufacturers also carry liability for errors in these documents and adopt slowly.

**2. A compliance desk for suppliers** that answers their customers' chemical and conflict-mineral questionnaires (the PFAS, REACH and conflict-minerals templates) from one set of stored product data.
- *Why it could work:* one mid-sized component maker can get requests from 40–50 customers a year across different portals, costing hundreds of hours. The tools I found (Assent, Certivo, Regilient) serve the buyer sending the requests. The supplier answering them seems underserved.
- *Weakest:* urgency is low. Supplier response rates run below 60%, which suggests many suppliers simply ignore the requests. Much of the pressure comes from reporting deadlines, and those keep moving. It could later fold into runner-up 1.

## Looked at and dropped

- **Recovering import duties on goods later exported (duty drawback):** there is plenty of unclaimed money (CBP estimates about $15B a year), but it's now crowded. Zollback is AI-native and VC-backed; Charter Brokerage and J.M. Rodgers are established. It also swings with tariff policy.
- **AI farmland appraisal:** Acres.com already has AI tools for appraisers, and Farm Credit lenders keep appraisal teams in-house.
- **PFAS reporting alone:** crowded, and the deadlines keep shifting.
- **Warranty for car dealers:** already taken by Forge, Reflolabs and WarrantyWriter.

## Before committing to the top idea (first 2–3 weeks)

Interview 10+ dealer service managers and warranty clerks. Get real rejection and charge-back numbers from three dealers. Ask directly whether they would pay a cut of recovered money. Check the portal-access terms for one manufacturer. If rejections are small, or dealers say they already recover nearly everything, drop the idea and move to runner-up 1.
