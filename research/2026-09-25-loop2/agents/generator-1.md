I recommend an AI agent that gets equipment dealers paid on warranty claims. It would start with farm and construction equipment, heavy-truck and RV dealers in the Midwest. I also have three runners-up. One limit up front: my web search allowance ran out partway through. So my check for competitors outside car dealers is incomplete, and it's the first thing to verify.

## Best idea: an AI agent that recovers warranty money for equipment dealers

**What it is:** When a dealer repairs a machine under warranty, it has to file a claim with the manufacturer to get paid. The agent reads the repair order, the technician's notes and photos. It rewrites the repair write-up in the wording each manufacturer's warranty manual requires, codes the claim, and files it in the manufacturer's portal under the dealer's own login. It then tracks payment deadlines and drafts appeals for denied claims.

- **Pricing:**
  - A share (20–25%) of money recovered on the dealer's backlog of denied or underpaid claims. This proves the value with no upfront risk to the dealer.
  - Then a monthly or per-claim fee for handling new claims.
- **Later expansion:** everything else a manufacturer owes a dealer:
  - sales incentives and rebates
  - recall campaigns
  - parts returns
  - pre-delivery inspection payments
  - service contracts

**Why it could work, by the judging criteria:**
- **Customer need:** The money is large and measurable:
  - US manufacturers paid $30.4B in warranty claims in 2025.
  - Deere paid $1.33B, Caterpillar $836M, PACCAR $772M, AGCO $415M, Thor $241M and Winnebago $98M.
  - One vendor says up to 30% of claims fail on first submission, mostly over paperwork rather than bad repairs.
  - Dealers eat the cost of denied claims, and they're short of technicians and warranty staff.
- **Value over alternatives:** The same model is already proven at car dealers:
  - Armatus has served 7,500+ car dealers on contingency, reporting about $102K more per store per year from getting paid retail rates on warranty work.
  - WarrCloud has 1,000+ car dealers and claims a 15.1% rise in warranty billings.
  - Forge AI claims 16% more recovered revenue.
  - Nobody obvious does this for equipment, truck or RV dealers. Their alternatives are an in-house warranty clerk or eating the loss.
- **Market size:** Warranty money flowing through equipment, truck and RV dealers is roughly $5–7B a year in the US. That estimate is mine, built up from the company figures above. If dealers are leaving 10–15% on the table, recovery fees alone could reach about $100–250M a year in revenue. Adding the other money manufacturers owe dealers, plus car dealers later, makes it much bigger.
- **Low risk to start and a real edge for Veer and Cole:** A pilot needs almost no integration. A dealer hands over 12 months of denied claims, and the team shows dollars recovered within weeks. Customers are close:
  - Purdue's agriculture network
  - Indiana farm-equipment and truck dealer groups
  - the Elkhart RV cluster
  - A result like "we recovered $X for three Indiana dealers" is exactly what these judges score.

**What's weakest:**
1. **The equipment-only market may be too small** for venture investors. The big pool is car dealers, where WarrCloud, Forge and Reflolabs already operate. The "everything a manufacturer owes a dealer" story has to carry the pitch.
2. **Manufacturers hold the power.** They control the portals, audit dealers and can charge back payments. An AI that pushes claims too hard could look like fraud. It has to be sold as reducing chargebacks, with a human approving each claim.
3. **Integration and sales friction:** dealer software systems vary and are locked down, and dealer groups are consolidating, so sales cycles to big groups get longer.
4. **Manufacturers are buying AI to judge claims** (Circuitry.ai, Bruviti), which could turn this into an arms race.
5. **Evidence gaps:** the 15–16% uplift and 30% first-submission failure figures are vendors' own marketing. No study backs them.

**First things to check (this is all desk research, not customer validation):**
- Interview 10–15 service managers and warranty admins at Indiana equipment, truck and RV dealers. Ask for their denial rate, their appeal window and who does the filing today.
- Search for competitors outside car dealers, which I couldn't finish.
- Ask whether the major equipment makers' dealer agreements allow automated portal submission.

## Runners-up

**1. An AI "fractional quality engineer" for small auto and aerospace parts suppliers.** It drafts the paperwork suppliers owe their customers: new-part approval packages (PPAP), corrective-action reports (8D), failure-risk analyses (FMEA) and audit prep.
- **Why it could work:**
  - Quality engineers are scarce, and this documentation is mandatory, recurring and deadline-driven.
  - Indiana has a dense supplier base, and Purdue's Manufacturing Extension Partnership (MEP) could be a way to reach it.
  - Suppliers in these regulated supply chains number in the tens of thousands.
- **Weakest:**
  - The space is full of small AI tools (EasyPPAP, Supplios, Humble Operations, iFactory).
  - Established quality-software vendors are adding AI.
  - Small manufacturers spend little on software, and quality engineers are wary of AI on documents that carry liability.

**2. Proof of metal and country origin for mid-sized importing manufacturers.** It collects supplier evidence for the steel, aluminum and copper tariffs: where the metal was melted and poured, or smelted and cast. It also covers country of origin and USMCA certificates.
- **Why it could work:**
  - As of April 6, 2026, these metal tariffs apply to the full customs value of finished products.
  - Aluminum whose origin can't be reported pays 200%.
  - The Justice Department's trade-fraud push under the False Claims Act includes a record $549.5M settlement (May 2026), and whistleblowers can collect a share. Importers are scared.
  - Later, it can grow into a shared supplier compliance-data network.
- **Weakest:**
  - Tariff policy keeps changing. The Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs in February 2026, and the metal-tariff rules were rewritten again in April.
  - Well-funded competitors exist: Gaia Dynamics closed a $7M seed on September 16, 2026, and Assent and Z2Data are established.

**3. AI-native cost segregation studies** for real-estate investors and their accountants. These studies split a building into parts that can be written off faster for tax purposes.
- **Why it could work:** The 2025 tax law (OBBBA) permanently restored 100% bonus depreciation, so the return on each study jumped. Studies currently cost $5–15K from engineering firms.
- **Weakest:**
  - It's mostly a one-time purchase per property.
  - AI-native competitors already exist (Overline).
  - It needs an engineer to sign off, and it carries IRS audit risk.
  - It fits "B2B SaaS" less cleanly.
