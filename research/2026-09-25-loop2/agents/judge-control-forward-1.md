I'd back this, narrowly: as software that debt advisors use to package and send out deals, with funds paying for a matched deal inbox. The biggest unanswered question is whether anyone pays yet, and one short test answers it.

**Claim check.** The pitch matches **Forward** (useforward.co) almost exactly, so I treated Forward as this team. It is in Y Combinator's Fall 2026 batch, has 5 people, and is based in San Francisco and Sydney.
- **"Funds managing $20B use it":** The site says "25+ funds managing $20B in AUM." I could not verify the total. The named customers I could check are small or mid-sized. Hudson Cove manages about $1.04B according to its SEC filing. Arbitrium is an Australian mid-market credit fund writing A$20–80M loans. iPartners is an Australian wholesale credit platform whose size isn't published. So $20B needs a few larger funds I couldn't identify. The site also doesn't say whether "use" means paying for software or just receiving deals.
- **"Over $100M of deals originated":** Only the company states this. It doesn't say what counts as originated, or whether those deals closed because of the platform.
- **Where the customers are:** The founders come from Australian credit (Rixon Capital, Longreach, Kain Lawyers), and several named customers are Australian. Australia's private credit market is about A$200B according to ASIC's Report 814, and about half of it is real estate lending.
- **Pricing:** Not public, as the pitch says.

**Who else serves this customer:**
- **AI diligence tools for funds** (parsing documents and screening against a mandate): Arc, F2, BlueFlame, Rogo, 9fin, Octus. This layer is crowded and not a moat.
- **Monitoring loans after close:** 73 Strings, Resiliq, Cardo. Also crowded.
- **Networks that route deals to capital:** Axial serves the US lower middle market with more than 3,500 advisory firms and 3,000 buyers, but it is mostly M&A. Finitive is a private credit marketplace where borrowers pay and investors pay nothing. Lev does this for commercial real estate: data on 4,000+ lenders, $70M Series B plus $100M in debt.
- **Lev is the key precedent.** It started as a tech-enabled brokerage and shut that business down because "the market didn't want another brokerage." It then sold software to brokers and lenders instead.
- **Does an incumbent own the customer or the buying channel?** Only in part. Intapp DealCloud holds many funds' pipeline data. Axial owns the US lower-middle-market advisor network. Lev owns real estate debt. Nobody owns the advisor side of corporate and asset-backed private credit in Australia or the US lower middle market.

**1. Strongest version.** "Lev for non-real-estate private credit," starting in Australia and then the US lower middle market.
- **Supply side:** Debt advisors use it for free or cheaply. They upload a borrower's materials once and get a standard deal package plus a list of funds whose mandates fit.
- **Demand side:** Funds under about $5B pay a subscription for a clean, mandate-matched deal inbox, with every claim linked to its source document.
- **The moat:** The standard deal package and the map of fund mandates, which build up the more both sides use it.
- **Cut for now:** The fee on each closed deal, because it puts the company in competition with the advisors who bring the deals. Also cut monitoring after close, which is already crowded.

**2. Ratings**
- **Customer need: 3.** Funds with capital to deploy want deal flow, and advisors shop deals by hand. The only evidence is the company's own logos and its "25+ hours per analyst, weekly" claim, with nothing independent.
- **Value over what customers use today: 3.** Today it's email, Excel and DealCloud. AI parsing is available from several vendors. The only new part is routing deals to matching funds, and that is unproven.
- **Market size: 3.** Software for a few thousand mid-sized credit managers and advisors is roughly a $0.5–1B market (my rough estimate). The larger pool, a fee on deal volume, conflicts with the advisors.
- **Risk: 2.** Both sides must be won at once. Funds are wary of deals that have been widely shopped. Lev shows the brokerage-fee model was rejected. Axial or DealCloud could add AI matching.

**3. What would kill it, and the fastest test.** It dies if funds take the free deal flow but won't pay for it, and advisors won't pay either. There would then be no revenue model except a deal fee that competes with the advisors.
- **Fastest test:** Offer a paid contract of about A$2–5k a month to the 25+ current fund users within two weeks.
- **Pass:** Paid conversion of at least 30%, plus proof that at least a few of the $100M in deals would not have reached those funds any other way.
- **Fail:** Under 10% conversion.
