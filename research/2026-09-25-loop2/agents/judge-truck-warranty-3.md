**Research summary.** The market numbers in the pitch are accurate. The tailwinds are weaker than the pitch says. The dealer-management systems already own the claim-submission rails, and at 1% the whole US truck market is only about $54M a year.

**Claims checked against primary sources**
- **$5.4B and $13,800 are correct.** NADA's *ATD Data 2025* reports warranty labor of $2.51B and warranty parts of $2.90B, which total $5.41B. It lists "service and parts sales per warranty repair order" at **$13,816**, and warranty at 10.2% of service, parts and body shop sales. The same report shows $47.9B of service and parts sales over about 2,100 dealerships ($22.77M each). That works out to roughly **$2.6M of warranty and about 190 warranty repair orders per dealership per year**. Each claim is large, but each dealership files few of them.
- **Separate maker portals: partly true.** Cummins uses its own RapidServe Web system and Allison uses its own AWAARE/HUB system. Detroit engine claims go through the truck maker's OWL system, and PACCAR's run through its own claims system. Eaton transmission claims go through the truck maker during the base warranty. The more a truck maker uses its own engines, the fewer portals a dealer deals with.
- **"Manufacturers are adopting AI": true.** In June 2024 Daimler Truck North America launched a warranty system built with Tavant that does "suspect claim scoring". That is the manufacturer auditing claims with AI. The same system also predicts repair codes for dealers, which does part of this product's job for free.
- **Tailwind removed.** In July 2026 the EPA proposed going back from the planned 10-year/450,000-mile emissions warranty to 5 years/100,000 miles. That cancels an expected jump in warranty volume.

**Who already serves this customer**
- **Dealer-management systems own the data and the submission rail.** Procede's Excede is the example Daimler Truck names for its required Detroit submission, through Excede's Warranty Claim Tool. Karmak Fusion builds claims from the repair order, with attachments, into Daimler Truck, PACCAR and Mack/Volvo systems. For farm and construction dealers, VitalEdge (IntelliDealer and e-Emphasys) plays the same role.
- **Outsourced claim processors** handle claims for dealers for 2–4% of paid claims. Examples are Warranty Consulting Services (heavy equipment, since 1997), Jupiter and Warranty Processing Inc.
- **AI tools for car dealers:** WarrCloud ($41.3M raised, 400+ dealers, AI plus human experts, submits claims itself), Claimlane (from $800/month, claims 5–15% more approved claim revenue), Forge AI, and WarrantyWriter (a $10–50/month tool that writes the technician's repair story).
- **No exact match found.** I found no company doing this for truck dealers.

**1. Strongest version**
A warranty recovery service for multi-brand truck dealer groups such as Rush (140+ locations), MHC and TEC (30+):
- **Start narrow:** big-ticket engine, aftertreatment and transmission claims to Cummins, Allison and one truck maker. One Cummins rulebook covers dealers of every truck brand.
- **Don't fight the systems:** read repair orders and attachments from Excede and Karmak rather than competing with them.
- **Sell the sharp part:** check each claim for missing evidence before submission, recover diagnostic time dealers leave unclaimed, and defend claims when the maker audits or charges them back.
- **Price on uplift:** charge a share of the extra dollars recovered compared with the dealer's baseline, not 1% of everything. That way it replaces the 2–4% outsourcers and doesn't have to justify a tax on claims that would have been paid anyway.
- **Expand later** to farm and construction dealers.

**2. Ratings**
- **Customer need: 3.** One rejected claim costs about $13.8K, and Daimler Truck now scores suspect claims with AI. But I found no primary data on how much truck dealers lose to rejections and chargebacks.
- **Value over today: 3.** The dealer-management systems move the data but don't check evidence against each maker's rules or draft appeals. Outsourcers do that with people at 2–4%. The extra value is real but unproven.
- **Market size: 2.** 1% of $5.41B is about $54M for US trucks. Farm and construction roughly doubles that. The car-dealer market is bigger but already has a well-funded player.
- **Risk: 2.** Procede and Karmak control data access and the submission integrations. Makers can build dealer-side AI, as Daimler Truck and Tavant already have. AI-written repair stories create fraud exposure when a maker audits the claim.

Total: 10 out of 20.

**Does an incumbent own the data or buying channel?** Yes. Procede and Karmak own the repair-order data and the connections to each maker's claim system. The makers own the portals and the rules.

**3. What would kill it, and the fastest test**
- **What kills it:** the losses it can fix are small. If rejections, reduced payments, chargebacks and unclaimed time come to under about 2–3% of warranty dollars, a 1% fee takes most of the gain. Mechanical claim entry is already handled by the systems above. A second killer is Procede or Karmak refusing API access, or copying the feature.
- **Fastest test (about 1 week):** get 90 days of maker claim statements (paid, adjusted, rejected, debited) from about 5 truck dealer locations. Add up the dollars lost for documentation reasons as a share of warranty dollars. Then run the rejected claims through a prototype to see how many it would have caught.
