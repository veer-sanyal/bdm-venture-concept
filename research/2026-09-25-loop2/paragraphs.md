## Controls

**C-perit (Perit.AI).** Pricing is not published; the last sentence gives the usual model for training-data vendors.

> AI labs training speech, video and robotics models are short on real-world data that shows how skilled people actually do a job: natural speech in many languages, first-person video of real tasks, human-guided robot demonstrations, and tactile and hand-pose streams. Scraped web data doesn't cover it, and generic labeling marketplaces use crowd workers rather than practitioners. This company records data from people who do the work, in nine languages so far, labels it with its own team, and delivers it with consent and license documentation for each file. It also runs public benchmarks for transcription and word-alignment models and builds reinforcement-learning environments, such as live voice calls with people trained to interrupt. Customers are AI labs and robotics teams. Revenue comes from collection projects and licensed datasets, priced per hour of data or per project.

**C-forward (Forward).** Pricing is not published; the last sentence gives the usual model for debt-origination platforms.

> Private credit funds now lend to mid-sized companies at scale, but deals reach them through commercial debt advisors as unstructured piles of financials, bank statements and proposals. Analysts spend days turning each pile into something a fund can screen, and many deals go to funds whose mandate never fit. This AI platform takes the borrower's documents once, extracts the financials, proposes a deal structure and covenants, and routes the packaged deal to funds whose mandates match it, with risks and open diligence items linked to the source documents. Agents then track each deal's progress and, after closing, flag covenant and repayment risks early. Customers are debt advisors and private credit funds. It says funds with $20B of assets use it. Revenue comes from fund subscriptions plus a fee on deals that close.

**C-vorelios (Vorelios).** Pricing is not published; the last sentence gives the usual model for simulation software.

> Engineers designing aircraft parts, cars, turbines and similar products depend on physics simulation, and one high-fidelity run can take hours or days. Because every design costs another solve, teams check a handful of options and never explore most of the design space. This company is training one foundation model on simulation data at scale so that it predicts a design's physical behavior in seconds instead of solving the equations each time. It starts with aerodynamics and plans to extend to other physics. The model is differentiable, so it can point a design toward better performance, and conventional solvers are kept for verifying the final candidates. Customers are engineering teams at manufacturers and design firms whose work is limited by solver time. Revenue comes from licenses or usage-based access to the model.

## Candidate

**K-airpermit (AI environmental department for small permitted plants).** Written by the orchestrator from the shaper's report. The Indiana FESOP fee ($6,100) was checked against IDEM's fee page. The 30% enforcement figure is the shaper's own count from EPA ICIS-Air bulk data (186 of 623 operating Indiana synthetic-minor plants, five years) and was not re-run.

> Small US manufacturers holding state air permits, such as coating lines, printers, plastics molders and metal finishers, must turn a long permit into deadlines, keep monthly emission records calculated from material purchases and safety data sheets, and file quarterly, annual and renewal reports. Plants without environmental staff pay consultants $1,500 to $20,000 a year or leave it to the plant manager. EPA enforcement data show about 30% of Indiana's synthetic-minor permit holders received a formal enforcement action in the last five years, most settling for small penalties. This company acts as the environmental department for those plants. It reads the permit, takes in monthly purchase and production records, calculates emissions and drafts every report for the plant official to sign, with an environmental engineer reviewing each filing. It starts with air permits in Indiana, then adds stormwater, spill plans, chemical inventories and hazardous waste. Plants pay $6,000 to $12,000 a year per facility.
