# Verify: load-tender accept/reject + driver assignment for small carriers

Researcher pass, 2026-09-21. Scope: AI B2B SaaS, Jev (TypeSafe AI) as the underlying model, sold to 1-20 truck carriers.

## Strongest support

- Tenders genuinely expire fast and automatically, which is the precondition for "needs a machine, not a human, to answer in time." Truckstop's own developer docs define a `LOADEXPIRED` webhook event: "Load posted from integrated application was not booked before the late pickup datetime and has expired" (https://developer.truckstop.com/reference/overview-2). Industry EDI guides describe the same mechanic on the contract-freight side: a carrier must answer an EDI 204 tender with an EDI 990 accept/reject inside a shipper-set window, commonly 15 minutes to 2 hours, or the tender auto-revokes to the next carrier in the routing guide (https://www.cleo.com/edi-transactions/edi-990, https://www.stacksync.com/edi/capstone/990, cross-checked against Best Buy's own EDI 990 implementation guide requiring a response "no later than 90 minutes from issue date and time," https://partners.bestbuy.com/documents/20126/104763/990+Response+to+a+Load+Tender.pdf).
- The money per decision is real and published. DAT's own weekly Dry Van Report states: "Dry van spot linehaul averaged $2.32 per mile this week, minus fuel" (week of 2026-07-31; https://www.dat.com/blog/dry-van-report-spot-rates-ease-as-summer-freight-demand-softens), with the 35-day forecast for late September 2026 near $2.19-2.24/mile per DAT's own trendline coverage. Detention pay guides put driver/owner-operator detention around $50-100/hour after a ~2-hour free period (https://otrucking.com/resources/guides/detention-time-and-pay/). A wrong accept/reject or a bad driver match on a 500-mile load is roughly a $1,000-1,200 gross-revenue swing before detention, deadhead, or a blown appointment are even counted.
- No incumbent small-carrier product currently outputs a calibrated probability for the decision itself. The closest thing found, McLeod's RespondAI (an enterprise TMS/brokerage add-on), automates rate quoting and order creation from inbound email/EDI but the vendor's own language is about drafting responses and quotes, not scoring accept/reject: "generate quotes or initiate orders directly from incoming emails" (https://www.mcleodsoftware.com/solutions/trucking-rate-management-software-respondai/); trade coverage confirms it "recommends a rate, formats a response, and allows the user to send or edit" (https://www.ccjdigital.com/technology/artificial-intelligence/article/15756024/mcleod-software-rolls-out-respondai-communication-automation). That is a drafting assistant sitting on top of a human decision, not a decision engine. McLeod's own product pages are built for PowerBroker/LoadMaster customers (mid-to-large brokers and asset carriers with dedicated IT), not the 1-20 truck segment this concept targets, inference from product positioning, not a quoted line, so flagged as such.

## Closest substitute: what the customer does today, and what stays unsatisfactory

Two real substitutes exist, both confirmed from primary pages:

1. **Human dispatch-for-hire, paid a percentage of the load.** TruckLeap's own pricing page: "5% of gross load revenue for most owner-operators - and just 4% for fleets," rising to 7% for new authorities, covering "load sourcing, rate negotiation, all broker calls, deadhead planning, and weekly reports" (https://truckleap.com/dispatch/pricing). This is a real person applying judgment and a rate-per-mile heuristic, the same process the brief says the product replaces, the dispatch service *is* the incumbent workflow, just outsourced instead of done by the owner-operator. It stays unsatisfactory because it costs 4-10% of every load's gross revenue forever (industry-standard range confirmed across three independent guides, e.g. https://idispatchhub.com/what-percentage-do-freight-dispatchers-charge-2026/), is still a human working from experience and rules of thumb rather than an outcome-trained model, and the dispatcher's incentives are not perfectly aligned (they are paid on booking activity, not on the carrier's realized margin, detention, or on-time record).

2. **One-click "book it now" or basic TMS accept/reject, with no scoring.** Truckbase (a real small-carrier TMS) offers "one-click 'Accept' or 'Reject' options" once a tender is EDI-integrated (per search-indexed description of Truckbase's EDI blog content; I could not independently re-confirm this exact sentence on the live page during this pass, so treat as NOT VERIFIED against the primary page directly, only against a search engine's cached summary of it). Truckstop's Book It Now similarly lets a carrier "book a load in a few fast clicks" once a broker has posted fixed terms (https://truckstop.com/product/load-board/book-it-now/) but the decision to click is still the dispatcher's, made "by eye." Neither product returns a probability, a margin estimate, or a driver-match recommendation; they only remove the phone call.

A general-purpose LLM given the same tender text and JSON state today could already produce a structured accept/reject with reasoning (function calling / structured output is a mature, well-documented capability across major model providers), but it would not be RLCD-calibrated, would cost far more per call, and per TypeSafe's own published comparison runs 3-329 seconds versus Jev's 70-500ms, per MarkTechPost's write-up of TypeSafe's launch figures (https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/09/19/typesafe-ai-releases-jev/). This general-model alternative is a reasoning inference on my part, not a claim I found published anywhere with a trucking-specific benchmark, flagged as inference, not verified fact.

## Important objection

Jev's calibration is unproven, by TypeSafe's own admission, and trucking accept/reject is exactly the kind of domain where miscalibration is expensive and silent. MarkTechPost's coverage of the launch is explicit: "'Zero hallucinations' means schema matching is guaranteed. The 0% figure is not empirical" (https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/09/19/typesafe-ai-releases-jev/), and a separate outlet frames the whole launch skeptically: "TypeSafe AI Raises $40 Million for Jev, but Its 445x Cost Claim Is Still Self-Tested" (https://ts2.tech/en/typesafe-ai-raises-40-million-for-jev-but-its-445x-cost-claim-is-still-self-tested/). The founders' own brief already flags this ("Its calibration on any domain is unproven"). Layered onto that: the product needs the carrier's live state (driver location, hours-of-service remaining, current commitments) as clean JSON on day one, and getting that requires either an ELD API integration (Motive's developer docs show OAuth-scoped access to `hos`, `vehicles`, `drivers`, `locations`, a real, gated integration a dispatcher-owner must authorize, https://developer.gomotive.com/, https://gomotive.com/legal/api-terms-of-service/) or manual entry, which is exactly the friction a one-or-two-person carrier is least likely to tolerate for a tool with no track record. If the model is wrong on a tender that had a tight window and a driver running low on hours, the failure mode (an accepted load that blows an HOS limit or misses an appointment) is worse than the status quo of a human saying no when unsure.

## Decisive unknown

Whether a dispatcher-owner at a 1-20 truck carrier will trust a probability from an unproven model on a decision with a real dollar consequence (roughly $1,000+ of gross revenue and a driver's HOS clock per load) enough to act on it without re-deriving the same judgment by eye anyway, and whether that trust could be earned fast enough, given that the model's stated edge (calibration proven against outcomes) requires weeks of the carrier's own accepted-load outcomes (margin, detention, on-time, broker payment) before it can plausibly outperform the owner's existing rule of thumb. Until then, the product's only edge over free tools (Book It Now, a load board's own filters) is a probability number nobody has reason yet to believe.

## Smallest useful next test

Approach one owner-operator or small-fleet dispatcher (1-20 trucks) who currently either self-dispatches or pays a percentage-based dispatch service, and ask to shadow the next 10-15 real tenders that arrive in a single week, by email, load board, or EDI. Record, for each: how the tender arrived, how many minutes were available to respond, what the dispatcher's own accept/reject and driver-assignment reasoning was (in their words, in real time), and the actual outcome (margin, detention, on-time, was the tender rate held or renegotiated). Compare that log against what a Jev-style call would have returned given only the same tender text and the carrier's self-reported state at that moment.

What would change the decision: if the dispatcher's real reasoning turns out to be dominated by a small number of legible factors (rate per mile versus a personal floor, deadhead miles, hours remaining, known-bad broker/lane) that a calibrated model could plausibly reproduce from JSON state alone, and if the dispatcher says they would have wanted a fast second opinion on at least some of those 10-15 calls, pursue it. If the real reasoning turns out to depend heavily on relationship knowledge (a specific broker's reputation, an off-book side deal, a driver's personal preference not captured anywhere) that never becomes clean JSON, or if the dispatcher says they would never act on a number they can't see the reasoning behind, that is the strongest reason to drop or re-scope the idea before building anything.

## What a new entrant would have that an incumbent would not

The model itself (Jev) is a commodity any incumbent, McLeod, Truckstop, Samsara, Motive, can buy at $0.042/M input tokens and wire into an existing tender-import pipeline faster than a two-person startup can acquire a single paying carrier. Nothing found in this pass suggests a new entrant has a durable technical edge. The only asset a new entrant could build that an incumbent starts without is a corpus of matched (tender, state, outcome) triples from a specific carrier's own loads, used to calibrate the model to that carrier's actual margins and broker relationships rather than a generic industry prior, and per the brief, Jev already claims to learn from exactly that kind of observed outcome. Whether that carrier-specific calibration loop is worth more than an incumbent's existing distribution (McLeod/Truckstop/Motive already sit inside the tender flow of most carriers who use any software at all) is unresolved and is itself downstream of the decisive unknown above.
