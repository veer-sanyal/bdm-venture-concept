# Fast-policy-from-state: six candidates (2026-09-21)

Urn: continuous decisions from live state that today run on hand-written rules, a heuristic, or a person watching a screen; graded by an outcome observed within minutes. Generated from the Jev model premise (calibrated probabilities/labels/scores, no reasoning or generation, text/JSON only, 70-500ms, $0.042/M input tokens). Hypotheses only, no invented customers or numbers.

## 1. Kitchen fire-and-pacing for independent restaurants

The customer is a single full-service restaurant (or small 3-5 unit group) whose expediter or kitchen manager decides by eye when to "fire" the next course for each table so apps, entrees and desserts land at the right spacing without cold plates or a table left waiting. The approach is a small screen next to the pass that ingests the POS ticket stream as it already exists (course, item, table, seat times, modifiers) and turns it into a per-table firing recommendation. The model's role: a yes/no question fired once per ticket-event ("fire item X now, given table Y's current course state and item Z's prep time") plus a labelled-option question when several items are contending for the line at once (which of up to N pending items should go next). It reads ticket text/JSON already in the POS, nothing new to capture. It could beat today's practice because a solo expediter tracks maybe 8-12 tables in their head under rush and drifts; a sub-second read of every open ticket does not drift and can be re-run every time state changes (a new ticket fires, a plate goes out). Biggest uncertainty: whether ticket timestamps and modifier text carry enough signal to distinguish "ready to fire" from "not yet" as well as an experienced expediter's peripheral vision of the actual line. Graded by ticket-to-table time and food-sitting time (plate up to table) per ticket, both already timestamped in most POS systems and visible within minutes of a shift.

## 2. Same-day job admission and technician assignment for small field-service shops

The customer is a home-services business with a handful of trucks (HVAC, plumbing, garage door, pest control) whose dispatcher decides, several times an hour, whether to slot a new same-day call and which tech gets it, currently by gut feel plus a paper or whiteboard board. The approach: read the dispatch software's live job state (each truck's current job text/notes, ETA, remaining jobs, skill tags) and the incoming call's text, and recommend acceptance and assignment before the dispatcher has to think about it. Model role: a yes/no ("can truck K realistically take this job today without blowing its next commitment") run against every truck, then a labelled-option pick among trucks that passed. It reads only what the scheduling tool already stores as text. Why better: a dispatcher juggling 6-10 trucks reasons from memory and a whiteboard, and under-books trucks defensively to avoid overpromising; a per-truck check run in under a second each time a call comes in can be re-evaluated continuously as jobs run long or finish early, which a person re-checks only occasionally. Biggest uncertainty: whether job-completion time is predictable enough from short text notes ("compressor replacement, older unit") for the yes/no to beat the dispatcher's own experience-based slack estimate. Graded by same-day jobs completed on time and trucks run over/under their day, both visible from the dispatch log within minutes of each job closing.

## 3. No-show-aware overbooking for a single-location dental or PT practice

The customer is a solo or small-group dental, PT or optometry practice whose front desk manually decides whether to double-book a slot against no-show risk, usually via a rough personal rule ("this patient reschedules a lot"). The approach reads the practice-management system's appointment record (patient history text: past no-shows, cancellation notice, appointment type, days since booked) each time a new slot is being filled or a gap opens, and recommends whether to double-book that slot. Model role: a yes/no probability of no-show for the specific patient/slot, refreshed the moment a booking or cancellation changes that day's schedule state; on a light day, a labelled-option question can also suggest which waitlist patient to pull into a freed slot. Why better: a receptionist may track a few "problem" patients from memory but cannot hold or recompute risk for 20+ slots a day the way a sub-second per-slot check can, and can re-run the check every time the schedule changes rather than only at booking. Biggest uncertainty: whether no-show probability is calibratable from the sparse fields most small practices actually keep (a handful of past visits) versus needing more history than a single-location practice has. Graded by chair-time actually filled vs. scheduled (utilization) and same-day gap minutes, both computable from the day's appointment log within minutes of close.

## 4. Dynamic admission and pricing for a single self-storage or event-parking facility

The customer is an independently operated self-storage facility or event-day parking lot whose manager currently sets one price a day (or a few tiers) and decides by feel when to stop discounting because a unit size or the lot is filling up. The approach reads the facility's live occupancy state (units/spots free by type, today's inquiry and booking text as reservations come in) and recommends, at each new inquiry, what to quote and whether to hold or release the last few units of a scarce type. Model role: a score (2-10 rubric) for "how tight is this unit type right now" translated to a quote tier, refreshed on every booking or cancellation, plus a yes/no admission gate for the very last few units when a lower-value long-shot inquiry arrives while a higher-value one might still show. Why better: a single manager only reprices when they happen to notice the board filling up, typically once a day; a check re-run on every inquiry adjusts within the same hour a unit type gets scarce. Biggest uncertainty: whether short inquiry/booking text carries enough signal to separate "genuinely scarce" from normal day-to-day noise in a facility this small, where sample sizes per unit type are thin. Graded by revenue per available unit/spot and units left empty at cutoff, both computable from the booking log within minutes of each transaction.

## 5. Ticket triage and escalation-before-breach for a small IT managed service provider

The customer is a small MSP (a handful of technicians serving 20-50 small-business clients) whose dispatcher currently triages incoming tickets by reading each one and guessing severity and who should own it, and often only notices an SLA is about to be missed when the timer nearly runs out. The approach reads each ticket's text plus the client's contract state (SLA tier, prior ticket history) the moment it lands or updates, and continuously recommends severity, routing, and an early escalation flag. Model role: a labelled-option question (severity tier, or which technician queue) on ticket creation, plus a yes/no re-run on a timer ("will this miss its SLA at current pace") that fires the anticipatory escalation before a human would think to check the clock. It reads only the ticket text and contract fields already in the helpdesk tool. Why better: a solo dispatcher triages in the order tickets arrive and checks SLA timers manually or via a static reminder; a policy that re-evaluates every open ticket every few minutes catches drift a person only samples. Biggest uncertainty: whether ticket text alone (no screenshots, no logs) carries enough signal for severity and time-to-resolve to route as well as a technician's first skim. Graded by SLA breaches per week and time-to-first-meaningful-response per ticket, both timestamped in the helpdesk system and visible within minutes.

## 6. Pick-wave release and order batching for a small 3PL or single-warehouse fulfillment operation

The customer is a small third-party fulfillment warehouse (one site, a handful of pickers) whose ops lead currently releases picking waves on a fixed schedule (e.g., every hour) or by eye when the queue "looks full," rather than continuously, because building a real wave-optimization system is more engineering than the business can justify. The approach reads the warehouse management system's live order queue (order lines, SKU locations, carrier cutoff times, picker availability, all as existing text/JSON) and decides, continuously, whether to release a new wave now and which pending orders to batch into it. Model role: a yes/no ("release a wave now, given current queue depth and time-to-cutoff") re-run every time an order lands or a picker frees up, plus a labelled-option split of eligible orders into batch groups by zone. Why better: fixed-interval releases either sit on orders that could ship sooner or dump too many at once and stall the floor; a check that re-runs on every state change reacts to the actual queue instead of the clock. Biggest uncertainty: whether the pick-time and routing signal available from order/SKU text alone (no live picker location) is enough to batch well without an existing slotting/routing engine underneath it. Graded by orders shipped per labor-hour and orders missing same-day cutoff, both readable from the WMS order log within minutes of each wave closing.
