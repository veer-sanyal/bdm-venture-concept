I recommend building one company, a reshaped version of idea 1 (Medicaid applications for nursing homes). I've added a weaker second as a fallback. The change-order idea (2) should be dropped: four funded companies moved into its pieces in 2026. All of this is desk research; nothing here has been checked with a customer.

## Company 1 (lead): an AI caseworker that gets long-term care paid for

**Customer and entry point.** Nursing-home operators in Indiana and the Midwest, starting with independent homes and small chains. The first job is the nursing-home Medicaid application, which takes about 30 to 60 days to assemble because of the five-year financial history. The product does the whole file:
- Chases the family by text and voice agent until every bank statement is in.
- Reads the statements and flags the transfers the state will question.
- Picks the right month to file, so the case stays inside the retroactive coverage window.
- Fills the state form and drafts the explanation letters.
- Answers each request for more information before its deadline, then handles yearly renewals.

**Why now**
- From 1 January 2027 the 2025 budget law cuts retroactive coverage for nursing-home applicants from three months to two. Once an application is filed, every month it slips beyond that is care the home may never be paid for.
- Slow processing is already political. Providers in some states have lost about $250,000 on average to slow applications; one lost $935,000. New Hampshire added $6M to its budget to speed up long-term-care cases (McKnight's).
- One correction to the generators' evidence: the Indiana "hundreds of millions" owed is $462M in delayed supplemental payments to 496 homes, held up by federal approval. It is not a backlog of applications, so don't cite it as one.

**The gap it fills**
- **CoreCare** (1,000 to 1,500+ locations, with state-specific products for Ohio and Texas) tracks pending cases, days pending and balances owed. Its public materials don't say it gathers family records or analyses the five-year history.
- **ExaCare** ($30M Series A, October 2025) covers admissions, billing documentation, surveys and clinical work, not eligibility files.
- The outsourced firms (Medicaid Done Right with 51 to 200 staff, Senior Planning Services, Medicaid Plus, Richter) do exactly this job with people.
- So the pitch is software doing the work those service firms staff by hand, not another tracking dashboard.
- I found no AI-native startup doing the family-records side. The nearest are Waterlily (planning for families) and Fortuna (general Medicaid enrollment for hospitals and plans).

**Answering why the earlier round stopped it**
- **Market size.** The earlier estimate was about $225M. My own rough figures, none verified: roughly 750,000 Medicaid nursing-home residents means roughly 300,000 to 450,000 new applications and 750,000 renewals a year. That puts the nursing-home slice at about $0.5 to 1B.
- **The billions come from expansion,** in this order:
  1. Home- and community-based Medicaid, and assisted living, which use the same financial-records engine.
  2. The VA Aid and Attendance pension. It has a 36-month look-back, and claims usually take 3 to 12 months or more.
  3. Long-term-care insurance claims: $14.1B paid in 2023 to about 353,000 people, projected to peak at $42B a year around 2041.
  4. Hospitals, for patients who are stuck waiting on long-term-care Medicaid, and then hospital eligibility work generally, where firms already charge contingency fees.
- The pitch becomes "every paperwork-heavy source that pays for a senior's long-term care", with the nursing-home application as the way in.
- **The competitor threat is real.** CoreCare or ExaCare could add this. The defence is doing the work and being paid per approved case, not selling seats.

**Pricing.** Per approved case or per bed per month, set against the $8,000 to $9,000 of unpaid care per resident per month. What the service firms charge is not published and needs finding out.

**Risks**
- If slow state offices are the real bottleneck, software doesn't fix it.
- Advising families on assets can count as practising law, so the product must stay on documentation, not planning.
- Health-privacy and bank-data security.
- Buyers have thin margins.
- There are 50 different state programs.

**Fastest tests**
1. Get two or three Indiana business-office managers to sort 100 closed pending cases by cause of delay: family, missing statements, state processing, or appeal. If most delay is the state, stop.
2. Ask one of the service firms, or a home that uses one, what it pays per case.

## Company 2 (fallback, lower confidence): fighting warranty chargebacks for suppliers

This reshapes idea 4 (dealer warranty claims). Instead of dealers claiming from manufacturers, it serves parts suppliers disputing the warranty and quality charges that manufacturers deduct from their payments.
- US-based auto suppliers paid $468M in warranty claims in Q3 2023, about $1.9B a year. That is roughly a tenth of industry warranty cost while they earn more than a third of its revenue (Warranty Week).
- Manufacturers are trying to recover more from suppliers. Warranty Week put the gap at about $2.5B a year in 2019, and GM recovered $2.7B from suppliers over the Bolt battery.
- Many manufacturers deduct the claim before the supplier has reviewed it (Claimlane). Some default to splitting costs 50/50 even when fault is unclear (Plante Moran).
- Indiana is dense with auto and RV component suppliers.
- **Weaknesses:** the slice I can see is small, perhaps $100 to 200M of contingency revenue in US auto before other industries. I found no data on how many "no fault found" returns are winnable. I also can't confirm that suppliers pay people specifically to fight these charges.
- **Fastest test:** ask three supplier warranty managers what share of last year's charges they contested, and what share they won.

## Dropped
- **Change-order recovery (idea 2).** Every piece of it now has a funded owner:
  - Notice deadlines: Document Crunch, which Trimble agreed to buy in April 2026.
  - Spotting drawing changes: Trunk Tools, whose Cortex tool launched in June 2026.
  - Pricing and time-and-materials tags for subs: Clearstory's AI agents, in closed beta from May 2026.
  - Forgotten change-order billing: Adaptive, $30M Series B in September 2026.
  - Billing and lien waivers: Siteline.
  - A pivot to retainage and closeout is a feature for Siteline, not a company.
- **Import duties (3), property tax (5), container demurrage (6).** Crowded or small, as the generators said.
- **Buyer-side sales-tax refund recovery,** which I tested as a replacement. It's already served by contingency firms (Ryan bought Avalara's recovery business in 2023) and by a Penn State student startup, Saveware.
- **Medicaid work-requirement enrollment.** Fortuna and others already serve providers.
