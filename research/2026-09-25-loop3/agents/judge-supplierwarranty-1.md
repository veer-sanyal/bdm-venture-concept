**Checking the pitch's claims**
- **The $468M figure is accurate but doesn't measure this market.** US-based parts suppliers did pay $468M in warranty claims in Q3 2023 ([Warranty Week](https://www.warrantyweek.com/archive/ww20231214.html)). But that group of 126 companies is led by Cummins, O'Reilly (a parts retailer), Wabtec and Eaton. It is mostly their own product warranties, not charges from automakers. In 2025 the group paid $2.18B, and Cummins alone paid $730M ([Warranty Week](https://www.warrantyweek.com/archive/ww20260604.html)).
- **A better size estimate:** suppliers pay roughly 10–15% of the industry's warranty bill ([Warranty Week 2019](https://www.warrantyweek.com/archive/ww20190418.html)). Ford, GM and Tesla together paid $11.75B in claims in 2024 ([Warranty Week](https://www.warrantyweek.com/archive/ww20250227.html)). That puts charges to suppliers from North American automakers at roughly $2–3B a year. That is my estimate, and most of it lands on large suppliers.
- **The "50/50 split" quote is accurate but old.** It comes from Plante Moran, April 2014 ([Plante Moran](https://www.plantemoran.com/explore-our-thinking/insight/2014/06/warranty-claims-a-hidden-financial-risk-for-auto-suppliers)), and so do the points on ineligible parts, excess labor and duplicate repairs.
- **Many charges are hard to call "wrong."** Supply contracts often make the supplier pay regardless of fault, using a flat split, an extrapolated sample, or even charges for returned parts where no fault was found ([Foley & Lardner, 2022](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/contracts-and-commercial-law/1174320/oems-expanding-suppliers-responsibility-for-ordinary-warranty-claims)).
- **The main fight is over one number, not each charge.** That number is the "technical factor": the share of claims the supplier is held responsible for. It is set from samples of returned parts and then negotiated. The main suppliers' association gives an example of a supplier charged at a default 60% while the real figure turned out to be 25% ([MEMA best-practices document](https://www.mema.org/system/files/2023%20Automotive%20Warranty%20Best%20Practices%20Final%20Document.pdf)). The same document says disputes lead to "tense commercial negotiations… even legal proceedings."
- **RV is a weak starting market.** RV component makers often honor their own warranties directly with dealers and owners ([Forest River FAQ](https://www.forestriverinc.com/rvs/faq)), so there are fewer charges from RV builders to dispute.

**Who else serves this customer**
- **Tools for the other side:** Tavant, Intellinet, NextGen, WarrantyHub and Claimlane help automakers and brands collect more from suppliers ([Intellinet](https://www.intellinetsystem.com/warranty-management-software), [Claimlane](https://www.claimlane.com/resources/blog/supplier-chargebacks-recovering-warranty-costs)).
- **Supplier-side help today:**
  - SAP's warranty module, which already handles technical-factor agreements for large suppliers ([Detering](https://www.deteringconsulting.com/blog/sap-automotive-supplier-fast-start)).
  - Human consultants such as Automotive Quality Consulting ([site](https://automotive-quality-consulting.com/en/claim-managements/)).
  - Law firms and accountants.
  - JD Power Repair Analytics, which sells warranty benchmarking to large suppliers ([JD Power](https://www.jdpower.com/business/repair-analytics)).
- **Who controls the data and the channel:** the automakers. Charges arrive through the automakers' own supplier portals. For large suppliers, SAP holds the internal workflow. Nobody owns mid-size suppliers' dispute work beyond Excel and consultants. I found no company pitching exactly this.

**1. The strongest version**
A warranty defense service for mid-size suppliers ($50M–$1B revenue) selling to automakers and to heavy-truck and off-highway equipment makers across the Midwest. It would replace RV in the starting market. It does three things:
- **Clerical cleanup of each charge:** parts the supplier doesn't make, duplicates, charges outside the warranty terms, labor outliers. This is the easy entry point.
- **Evidence for the technical-factor negotiation:** no-fault-found rates from returned-part analysis, the build dates when a fix took effect, and whether the automaker directed the supplier to use a particular sub-supplier. This is where the dollars are.
- **Recall cost-allocation files.**

Pricing: a contingency fee on money reversed plus on reductions in the technical factor. The same service later extends to other industries where buyers charge suppliers for quality failures.

**3. What would kill it, and the fastest test**
It dies if clearly disputable charges are a small share of what mid-size suppliers are billed (under about 5%), or if suppliers won't dispute them for fear of hurting the customer relationship.

The fastest test: under NDA, get the last 12 months of charge detail (an Excel export from the GM, Ford or Stellantis portals) from 3–5 Midwest supplier warranty managers. Run a free look-back cleanup in two weeks. Then ask each one whether they would actually file the flagged disputes on a contingency contract.
- **Keep going** if at least 8% of charges are disputable and at least 2 of the suppliers sign.
- **Stop** if it's under 5%, or if they won't file against their customer.
