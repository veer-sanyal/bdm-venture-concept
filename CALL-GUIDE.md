# CALL GUIDE. The ideas in plain words, who to call, what to ask, and why.

**Written 2026-09-07 for Veer and Cole. Owner of what the calls are FOR: `STATE.md`. Owner of how a
call is scored: `METHOD.md` Part 2b (the demand ladder) and Part 8 (the rubric).**

## Why the calls matter more than any more research

The judges score seven things. Three of the seven are about the customer and how badly they need this
(who exactly is the customer, what job are they trying to get done, how big and unmet is the need). Two
are about whether our answer is different and better. One is market size. One is risk.

Everything the desk can do has been done. About 55 ideas have been checked against contracts, fee
schedules, competitor product pages, patents and job postings. What no search can answer is the one
question the judges care about most: **does a real person in this job experience this as a problem worth
paying to fix, or as weather they stopped noticing years ago?**

The method calls that SALIENCE, and it is the only thing a phone call can settle. One rule: if two of
three people you call cannot remember the last time this happened to them, or shrug at the cost, the idea
is dead no matter how good the paperwork looks. That is the K4 kill, and it is the only kill that comes
from a customer instead of a desk.

**Bring back their words, not a paraphrase.** Write down the exact sentence. A quote from a real
contractor about what a failed inspection cost him in days scores higher than every competitor sweep in
this project.

## How to run every call (same for all four ideas)

1. Ask for 15 minutes. Say you are a Purdue student researching how their business handles one specific
   thing. Do not pitch. Do not describe the product until the end, if at all.
2. Open with the salience question first, before they know what you are hoping to hear:
   **"When did this last happen to you, and what did you do about it?"**
3. Then the numbered questions for that idea, in order. Each one is aimed at a specific hole the desk
   could not fill.
4. Close with: "Who else should I talk to about this?" Two names beats one call.
5. Within an hour, write down exact quotes, the numbers, and which questions changed which score. Hand
   that to the session and it goes into `STATE.md` as evidence.

Three calls per idea is the bar. One call is colour. Two is a hint. Three is evidence.

## Idea A. The subcontractor's deadline trap (construction claim notice)

**Rank after the 2026-09-07 re-score: 1st of six. Lowest score 2 (on "measured improvement"), total 23.**

### The idea, in plain words

Imagine you are an electrician company with 40 people. You do not work for the building owner. You work
for a bigger company, the general contractor, who works for the owner. When something goes wrong on the
job that is not your fault, like the owner changing the plans or another crew being late, you are
allowed to ask for more money or more time. But there is a catch hidden in your contract: **you have to
say so in writing within a certain number of days, and if you miss it, you lose the right to ask.
Forever.**

How many days? Nobody can look it up. We read eight real contracts filed with the SEC and the answer was
5 days in three of them, 48 hours in two, 14 days in one, and two had no deadline at all. Each one was
written differently. And often the clock in your contract points to a clock in the general contractor's
contract with the owner, which you may never have seen. So the real answer comes from reading two
contracts together and doing the arithmetic.

A Washington court in 2023 threw out a subcontractor's claim for $1.56 million because they did not send
the notice in time. The court said it did not matter that the general contractor obviously knew about
the problem.

**The product:** when something goes wrong on a job, you tell it what happened and when. It reads your
subcontract and the prime contract and tells you: your deadline is this date, here is why, and if you
miss it here is what you lose. Nobody sells this. Free tools exist for a different kind of notice (lien
notices) because those deadlines are written in state law and can be looked up. This one cannot.

### What the desk could NOT settle, and why you are calling

The desk found the money is real (a court's $1.56M) and found nobody competes. What it could not find
is whether anyone at a 20 to 300 person subcontractor is actually paid to worry about this, and how often
it happens. At big subcontractors (400+ people) there are contract administrators, but their job
descriptions cover lien notices, never claim notices. At small ones we could not read the job postings.
The worry is that this task only fires when something goes wrong, and companies fund seats for things
that happen on every project, not for emergencies.

### Who to call

An owner, project manager or estimator at an electrical, mechanical, plumbing or fire-protection
subcontractor with roughly 20 to 300 employees. Lafayette and Indianapolis are full of them. Cole may
know one. A Purdue construction management professor can name three.

### The questions, in order

1. **"When did a change or a delay last hit one of your jobs, and did you get written notice to the GC
   before the deadline in your subcontract? What did it cost you when you did not?"**
   *Why:* this is the salience test. A dated story with a dollar figure raises the "unmet need" score to
   4 out of 5. If two of three shrug, the idea dies.
2. **"Who in your company reads the claims clause of the subcontract when something goes wrong? How many
   hours a year does that take, and does anyone else look at it?"**
   *Why:* this settles whether anyone is paid to do this at their size. A named person with real hours is
   good news. "Nobody, we just call the GC" means the market is unpaid, and the score drops hard unless
   they can also name a claim they lost that was worth more than that person's time.
3. **"How many times a year on a typical job does something happen that you could claim for? And would
   you pay per project to have the deadline and the forfeit answer worked out the day you sign?"**
   *Why:* we have no frequency number anywhere. Above one event per job, and a price above about $700
   (what a law firm charges to read a contract once), turns "42,774 firms" into a dollar market. Under
   $100 or "we would just ask the GC" lowers two scores.
4. **"Have you ever lost a claim, or given up on one, because of a notice deadline?"**
   *Why:* if the answer is yes anywhere in three calls, that sentence goes in the pitch verbatim.

### What we are hoping to hear vs what would hurt

- Helps: "It happened on the hospital job last spring, we ate about $40,000." "I read those clauses
  myself at night." "Every job has two or three of these."
- Hurts: "Never been a problem, the GC always works with us." "We have a lawyer for that." "We do not
  bother, it would sour the relationship."

## Idea B. Fighting the charge that comes back from your customer (supplier chargeback defense)

**Rank after the re-score: 2nd of six. Lowest score 2 (on four criteria), total 18. The biggest
question mark is legal, not commercial: the car companies' purchase terms forbid pooling their data,
even anonymised, so the product must work for one supplier at a time.**

### The idea, in plain words

Imagine you run a small factory that makes metal parts for a bigger factory that makes car seats. One
day a bill shows up from your customer: "Your parts were bad. We had to stop our line, hire people to
sort through your boxes, and ship replacements by air. You owe us $6,200." This is called a chargeback,
and they do not send it as a request. **They take the money out of what they already owe you, unless
you object within a set number of days with the right documents.** Adient gives you 30 days. Piston
gives you 10. Miss the window and it is gone.

Most small suppliers either miss the window or write back with a story instead of proof. The proof that
works is boring: your shift records showing the sort hours claimed are impossible, the carrier's
timestamp showing the freight was not actually expedited, the inspection log for that lot.

**The product:** the chargeback arrives, you upload it plus your own production, inspection and shipping
records for that lot. It tells you which lines to fight, with which document, and which to concede.
Then it files the response inside the window. Every customer publishes a fixed admin fee ($200 to $275),
and the big money is the pass-through charges on top.

### What the desk could NOT settle, and why you are calling

Three things, and the first one can kill the idea by itself. (1) When a chargeback is partly reversed,
does the credit come back line by line, or as one negotiated number? If it is one number, we can never
learn which document won. (2) Who actually decides the dispute? The quality manual says plant Quality
decides, but Adient escalates disputes to the Purchasing person who awards the next contract. If disputes
are decided on relationship rather than evidence, the whole premise is wrong. (3) How many of these does
a supplier get a year and what does the worst one cost all in? Not published anywhere.

### Who to call

The quality manager or plant manager at a Tier-2 or Tier-3 supplier, 20 to 300 people, that sells into
automotive, appliance or heavy equipment. Indiana has hundreds: the Subaru and Caterpillar supply bases,
the Kokomo and Fort Wayne corridors. A family contact in the Samsung supply base is not this customer, but may know one. The
Purdue MEP center (Manufacturing Extension Partnership) talks to exactly these companies every week and
would give you a name.

### The questions, in order

1. **"When did your last chargeback arrive, what was the total including the sort, downtime and freight
   lines, how many did you get last year, and what did you do?"**
   *Why:* salience plus the two numbers nobody publishes. Monthly, in the thousands, raises the "unmet
   need" score to 4. "A couple a year at $250, we just pay them" is the kill.
2. **"When the last one was partly reversed, did the credit come back line by line, or as one number?
   And who made that call, plant Quality or Purchasing?"**
   *Why:* line by line on evidence means the product can learn. One number decided by Purchasing means
   the product is arguing with a relationship, and the "performance" score falls to 1.
3. **"How often do you look at a debit and decide the fight is not worth the relationship? Is there a
   dollar line below which you never dispute?"**
   *Why:* this tells us where the money actually is, and whether a tool that says "dispute more" would be
   used at all.
4. **"Have you ever sent a chargeback or debit memo to anyone outside the company, an accountant, a
   consultant, your ERP vendor? Did the customer's purchase terms ever come up?"**
   *Why:* the legal question. If suppliers routinely share these with outside firms and nobody has ever
   objected, the single-supplier product is safe. "We cannot, the PO forbids it" would threaten even that.

   **⚠ ASK WHICH OEM FIRST, added 2026-09-11 after the instrument check.** The answer differs by customer
   and it is not a detail. **Ford §16.03 expressly permits sharing with "consultants, contractors, experts,
   and agents" under a written confidentiality flow-down. GM's §30 has no such carve-out and names
   subcontractors inside the bar.** So a Ford-supplying shop can lawfully hand documents to a vendor and a
   GM-supplying shop cannot, which is the reverse of what this project assumed for a week. **Ford-supplying
   suppliers are the easier first customers; find out which side of that line you are on before asking for
   files.**

4b. **"Take a batch you shipped three weeks ago. Where would you find its shift records, its inspection
   log, and the carrier's pickup time, and how long would it take to pull all three?"** Added 2026-09-12.
   *Why:* tells us which system holds each record (ERP, spreadsheet, paper binder), which sets how
   onboarding works, and it measures the gathering time the product saves. "Twenty minutes, it is all in
   Plex" means gathering is not the pain. "Half a day, and the binder is at the other plant" is the pain.

4c. **"Besides quality chargebacks, what else does that customer debit you for? Late deliveries, warranty
   returns, controlled shipping? Same portal, same dispute window?"** Added 2026-09-12.
   *Why:* market size is a 2, and the expansion story has to be evidence, not assertion. Delivery Problem
   Notices are verified at Piston; warranty and controlled-shipping debits are not. "Same portal, same
   window" for two or more means the product grows without changing shape.

4d. **"Is your customer charging back more than it did two years ago? Has anything changed since the
   tariffs?"** Added 2026-09-13.
   *Why:* the desk found the customer base flat but supplier cost pressure sharply up in 2025-2026 (MEMA
   Q4 2025 cost index 91; OEMs delaying tariff cost recovery). Whether that pressure shows up as more or
   bigger QUALITY chargebacks is an inference no source confirms. A yes is the pitch's "why now"; a no
   means the timing argument cannot be made.

### What we are hoping to hear vs what would hurt

- Helps: "Got one last month for $11,000, half of it was sort labor we could have disproved." "We win
  about a third of what we fight." "Our controller sends them to our accountant all the time."
- Hurts: "We just pay them, it is the cost of doing business." "Purchasing calls and we settle at half."
  "We have never disputed one."

### ⚠ THE TWO ASKS. Added 2026-09-11 when Veer chose this idea. A question tests interest; an ASK tests demand.

**Every call ends with both, in this order, and the answers matter more than anything in the numbered
questions above.** Four rounds of this project proved the desk can establish that a mistake is expensive
and frequent. Only a person can establish that they will act.

5. **"Would you send me five closed chargeback cases, redacted however you want, so I can see what the
   winning and losing ones actually looked like?"**
   *Why:* this is the cheapest real commitment that exists. It costs them fifteen minutes and no money.
   **A supplier who will not hand over a redacted closed case does not have a problem they care about**,
   and it also answers question 4 by action rather than by opinion. If three suppliers send files, the
   back-test is buildable this month with no software written.
6. **"If I did the next one by hand, start to finish, and you kept whatever came back, would you pay for
   that?"**
   *Why:* the only question whose answer is money. A yes at any price is the finding. **Note what this is
   NOT: it is not a software sale and must not be pitched as one.** Doing one case by hand is how you find
   out whether the judgment is learnable at all, and the room's rubric has no traction criterion, so a
   paid manual pilot costs nothing in scoring and buys the criterion-3 answer outright.

7. **⚠ IF THE CALL GOES WELL: "Could I come see a sort? An hour, whenever one is running."** Added
   2026-09-11, when Veer decided against routing this through the SCOM field study. **That decision removed
   the only mechanism in the repo that put him physically inside a plant, so the ask has to be made
   directly instead.** It is a bigger commitment test than the redacted-files ask and it costs them
   nothing but a visitor badge. **And a sort is the mistake in physical form:** people at a table, a
   containment bin, a clock running, someone's premium freight on the way. **An hour watching one is worth
   more to the December pitch than any document**, because the rubric's three customer-facing categories
   are answered by what he saw and who he watched do it, and because no competing team will have stood in
   that room. A yes here also means the relationship survives past one phone call, which is what the paid
   pilot needs.

### ⚠ THE THIRD CALL, AND IT IS A DIFFERENT PERSON

**The gap this idea has that the others do not is DOMAIN CREDIBILITY, not evidence.** Judging which line
of a quality chargeback is defensible needs someone who has read a control plan, argued a PPAP, and sat on
the wrong end of a sort. **Veer does not have that and cannot acquire it by reading.** So one call in every
three goes to a possible advisor rather than a possible customer: a retired or semi-retired supplier
quality manager, an automotive quality consultant, or a Purdue faculty member in industrial or
manufacturing engineering. **The ask is one hour a month to tell you when you are wrong**, and the answer
also tells you whether the judgment is teachable to a model at all.

Purdue MEP is the strongest single channel for both calls at once: it works with exactly these companies
every week, it is federally subsidised so it is not selling him anything, and a Purdue student asking how
Indiana suppliers handle chargebacks is a call they take.

## Idea C. Knowing the day before whether the inspector will pass you (inspection readiness)

**Rank after the re-score: 4th of six. Lowest score 1 (on "unmet need"), total 15. It has the best data
story and the worst demand story on the board. It also collides with last year's winner, PipeLine, which
pitched "AI to reduce costly rework for trade contractors."**

### The idea, in plain words

When a contractor builds or fixes something, the city sends an inspector to check it before the next
step. If the work is not ready, or the paperwork is not on site, or the wrong inspection was booked, the
inspector leaves and you wait days or weeks for the next slot. Cleveland literally codes this in its
records as "Useless Trip / Work Not Ready." Some cities charge for it: Kingston, NY charges $250 the
first time, $500 the second, $1,000 the third.

We found that cities publish every inspection result, per permit, on their public portals, and that
contractors who failed before fail again at 3 to 10 times the rate of everyone else. **The product:**
give it your license number, and the day before each inspection it tells you whether you are actually
ready, based on what this city, this inspection type and this inspector have failed people on before.

### What the desk could NOT settle, and why you are calling

Nobody is employed to do this. We searched job postings and found "permit coordinator" roles where
scheduling inspections is one bullet among many, but no role whose job is making sure the inspection
passes. That usually means the pain is real but spread too thin for anyone to pay for. Also, we do not
know what a failed inspection costs the contractor in days and dollars, only what the city charges.

### Who to call

A contractor who pulls 20 or more permits a year: a residential remodeler, an HVAC or plumbing company,
a small GC. Ask around Lafayette. Any plumber who has done work for a friend's parents counts.

### The questions, in order

1. **"When did an inspection on one of your jobs last come back Not Ready or fail, and what did it cost
   you in days and dollars?"**
   *Why:* salience, and the contractor-side cost that no document publishes. A story with a day count
   raises the "unmet need" score from 1 to 3 or 4. "We rarely fail" is the kill.
2. **"Who decides the day before whether to keep or cancel the inspection? Is that anyone's actual job?"**
   *Why:* if a named person owns it, the market is paid and the score rises. "The foreman, nobody extra"
   confirms it is unpaid.
3. **"Do you use the city's free checklist, or one of those $99 virtual pre-inspection services? What did
   it miss last time?"**
   *Why:* the free and cheap alternatives are real. Their residue, in the contractor's own words, is what
   we would be selling.

## Idea D. The yearly report every drug factory must write (pharma annual product review)

**Rank after the re-score: tied 3rd of six. Lowest score 1 (on "unmet need"), total 15. It cleared every desk check but the product already
exists for big pharma; the open question is whether small contract manufacturers can buy it.**

### The idea, in plain words

Every factory that makes over-the-counter or generic medicine in the US must, by federal rule, write a
report once a year for every single product it makes, showing the quality data trended over the year
and whether anything drifted. FDA inspectors check for it, and about 7 to 8 percent of FDA write-ups to
drug factories cite this exact rule. A small contract manufacturer with 30 products writes 30 of these a
year, forever.

Big pharma buys software that pulls the numbers from their lab systems and computes the statistics
automatically. **Every named customer of that software is a large multi-site company.** The mid-size
quality systems that a 40 to 250 person contract manufacturer actually owns have no such feature at any
price. So the small ones are, we think, doing it in Excel.

### What the desk could NOT settle, and why you are calling

Whether the small manufacturers' batch data lives in a system at all, or on paper and in spreadsheets.
If it is paper, the product cannot ingest it and the idea dies. And whether anyone has ever tried to
sell them the enterprise software. If nobody has called them, the gap is real today; if the vendors have
called and been turned down, it is a pricing problem, not ours to solve.

### Who to call

The QA manager or head of quality at an OTC or generic solid-dose contract manufacturer with 40 to 250
employees. Indiana has several. LinkedIn search "Quality Assurance Manager" plus "contract manufacturing"
plus "OTC" within 100 miles of Indianapolis.

### The questions, in order

1. **"How do you produce your annual product reviews today, and where does the batch data physically
   live: a LIMS, an ERP, Excel, paper?"**
   *Why:* paper kills it. Excel is workable. A system is ideal.
2. **"How long does one take, who does it, and has FDA or a customer auditor ever written you up on
   one?"**
   *Why:* magnitude and salience. Hours per report times reports per year is the number we do not have.
3. **"Has anyone ever tried to sell you software for this? What happened?"**
   *Why:* tells us whether the gap is real or just priced out of reach.

## Two new ideas from tonight's round, ranked 2nd (tie) and 4th on one screen each

Both came out of a lane the search had never used: the customer is the STRONGER party in the
relationship, and the mistake is one they make that a court or a state office grades against them. Both
have had exactly one blind screen, so their ranks are provisional. If you know anyone in either job, one
call would settle more than another week of desk work.

### Idea E. Telling an HOA manager whether a fine will stick (HOA fine enforceability)

**Plain words.** Homeowners associations fine residents for things like an unapproved fence or a boat in
the driveway. But the association can only fine you if it followed its own rulebook and the state law:
the rule has to have been adopted properly, you have to get notice and a hearing, the fine schedule has
to be on record. If they skip a step and you fight it, the fine gets thrown out, and in Florida and
Arizona the association pays your lawyer. The manager who sent the letter is a licensed professional who
can be fined up to $5,000 per count in Florida for violating the HOA statute. **The product** reads the
association's own documents and the state law before the letter goes out and says: this one will not
hold up, here is what is missing.

**Weak spots the desk found.** Fines are capped at $100 each and $1,000 total in Florida and Nevada, so
the cheapest safe answer is "send a courtesy notice and never fine," which is free. The violation-tracking
software managers already use handles the deadlines and the arithmetic. And we could not find a single
case of a manager actually disciplined over a fine.

**Who to call.** A community association manager at a firm running 20 to 200 associations. Indiana has
them; Florida has thousands.

**Questions.** (1) When did a fine last get reversed in your portfolio, who paid, and how much? (2) Does
your violation software stop you from sending a notice when the rule is not in the adopted documents?
(3) What share of your associations actually fine versus courtesy-notice only, and does your lawyer look
at individual notices?

### Idea F. Telling a landlord which deposit deductions will survive (security-deposit defense)

**Plain words.** When a tenant moves out, the landlord can keep part of the deposit for damage, but not
for normal wear, and must send an itemized list on a deadline. If a tenant takes them to small claims
and wins, the landlord can owe two times the deposit in California or three times plus lawyer fees in
Texas and Massachusetts. Since 2025 California law also forces landlords to photograph the unit before
and after, so the evidence now exists by law. **The product** looks at the move-in and move-out photos,
the invoices and the state law, and says which deduction lines will survive a challenge and which to drop.

**Weak spots the desk found.** The free layer is strong (HUD publishes the depreciation chart, the
apartment association publishes the forms), a company called Roost already handles disputes for
operators above 500 units, and a deduction you drop is never tested, so the product cannot learn from
half its own answers. Nobody could confirm how often deductions are actually contested.

**Who to call.** A property manager at a 50 to 500 unit operator. Any Purdue student's landlord counts.

**Questions.** (1) In the last year, how many move-out statements were contested, how many went to court,
how many did you lose, and what did that cost? (2) Who decides each deduction line today and how long does
one statement take? (3) Have you ever dropped a deduction you thought was valid to avoid a 2x claim?
